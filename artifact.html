Track how well things are going for a story’s hero, moment by moment, and draw it as a line: beginning on the left, end on the right, up is good luck, down is bad. Thousands of very different stories draw the same few lines. These are those lines.

Part I — The idea

One line per story

In 1985 Kurt Vonnegut walked to a chalkboard and drew story after story as a single line — good fortune up, ill fortune down, beginning to end. His point: the specifics vanish and a handful of simple shapes remain. His joke with teeth: since the shapes are so simple, “there is no reason why they can’t be fed into computers.” Thirty years later, a team at the University of Vermont did exactly that.

Part II — The shapes

Seven shapes cover nearly everything

Six were confirmed by the Vermont team across 1,327 novels — three basic moves, each also possible upside down. The seventh is Vonnegut’s own exception: the story whose line refuses to be drawn. Counts are from this atlas’s 84 books.

Part III — The machine

How a computer read 1,327 books it couldn’t understand

A computer can’t follow a plot. But ten thousand common words have been rated for happiness by human readers — “laughter” scores 8.5 out of 9, “terrorist” 1.3. The fine print that matters: this line hears the mood of the words, not the luck of the hero. Most of the time they travel together. When they don’t — and we tested this — the word-line can be flat, or even upside down. That test is Part V.

Part IV — The atlas

Eighty-four books, one line each

Every line answers one question: how well are things going? Three kinds of line, told apart in plain words on each book: computed from the words (the Vermont method, applied to the original texts), drawn from a reading (by hand, the way Vonnegut did it), or scored by a reader (a machine reader that worked through the whole book in twenty slices, judging events — our best instrument).

Part V — A better reader

What happens when the reader actually understands the book

We took five books and scored them twice: once with the word-mood method, once with a machine reader that read all twenty slices of each book and judged the events. On four of the five, the two lines barely relate — and the failures cluster at endings, where weddings and redemptions are brief in words but enormous in meaning. The word-counter hears A Christmas Carol’s party scenes; it cannot hear that the last page is the whole point.

hero’s fortune, scored by reading events mood of the words (2016 method)

And the shapes themselves? Every reader-scored line still lands inside the same six families. The better instrument corrects which shape a book gets — it found no new shapes. Vonnegut’s alphabet survives.

Part VI — East and West

The oldest stories draw the same valley

We pointed the same reader at four Indian classics — two epics (condensed by Romesh Dutt in the 1890s), one Sanskrit play, one episode-tale. Same alphabet: deep valleys, falls and recoveries. Different signatures: the trapdoor is a dice game in both epics; battlefield victories score as misfortune (winning at Kurukshetra is a grief); and the Ramayana keeps filming after the happy ending — coronation at the peak, then Sita swallowed by the Earth. Where Western tellings stop the camera at the wedding, the epic lets the wheel keep turning.

One caveat we owe the material: Sanskrit dramaturgy consciously composes a sequence of emotions (the rasas), not one hero’s luck — so where an Indian text looks odd under this lens, part of that is the lens.

Part VII — The map

Every book in shape-space

Left–right: does the story end worse or better than it began? Up–down: is its middle a peak or a pit? The shape families fall out as neighborhoods.

Appendix

Method & sources

The three kinds of line

Computed from the words (31 books): per-book sentiment series from Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth & Dodds, “The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes” (EPJ Data Science, 2016), via their public core-stories data; labMT word-happiness lexicon (Dodds et al. 2011); smoothed, each book normalized to its own range. Orientation independently verified by re-deriving five books from raw Gutenberg text (all correlate positively).

Drawn from a reading (44 books): hand-scored fortune arcs against a shared calibration rubric — readings, not measurements, exactly what Vonnegut did at the board.

Scored by a reader (9 books): a machine reader split each full text into equal slices — twenty for novels and epics, sixteen for the two shorter works — read each slice, and scored the protagonist’s situation 1–9 from events, never from prose mood, recording the justifying event for every point (visible on hover).

What we verified ourselves

  • Re-ran the 2016 decomposition on the corpus: three basic curves ± their mirrors explain ~84% of all arc variance. The six shapes are real.
  • Word-mood vs reader-scored fortune on 5 books: near-zero agreement on 4 (r = −0.14…+0.16); Dorian Gray the exception (r = +0.90). Failures concentrate at endings.
  • No new shapes in 9 reader-scored stories, Western or Indian.
  • The “Ramayana” in one common anthology is Book One only — always check a text covers the full arc before trusting its line.
